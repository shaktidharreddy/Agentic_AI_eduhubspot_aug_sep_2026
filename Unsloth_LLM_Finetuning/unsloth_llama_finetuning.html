Part 1 · Concepts

The ideas behind fine-tuning

You can't fine-tune well until you can picture what is happening inside the model. These nine ideas are all you need.

Concept 1

What is fine-tuning? Moving skills you already have to a new game

The video starts with a story. Someone who grew up playing cricket moves to the US and is taken to play baseball. They are comfortable almost straight away, because both games need the same raw skills: watch the ball, swing the bat, run. They don't learn from zero. They transfer their skills and adjust a little.

LLMs work the same way. A model like Llama has already read a huge amount of internet text, so it knows grammar, facts and how to reason a little. Fine-tuning gives it a short extra round of training on your data so it picks up your topic, vocabulary, tone or answer format.

Pre-trained LLMKnows general language from huge internet datasets.
+
Your dataA PDF, company documents, Q&A pairs, chat logs…
→
Fine-tuned LLMSame model, now "speaks" your domain and style.
DefinitionFine-tuning means taking a model that is already trained and training it a bit more on a specific dataset, task, tone or format.
Concept 2

Why not just use the model as it is? RAG vs fine-tuning

The video uses a made-up company called Loki Phones. You're building its customer-support chatbot. Its plans and policies are private, so they were never on the internet and the base model has never seen them.

"My Loki Phone 12 screen is cracked. What are my options?"
Plain LLM
"You can take your phone to a repair shop or contact the manufacturer…"

Generic. It doesn't know the LokiCare+ plan exists.

RAG (looks up your docs)
"Per policy document 4.2: LokiCare+ covers screen replacement. Submit a claim."

Correct facts, but robotic, and not in your brand's voice.

Fine-tuned
"Oh no, sorry about your screen! Good news: if you have LokiCare+, a replacement is covered. Here's how to book it…"

Brand tone, empathy, your preferred format.

Illustrative answers. The idea comes from the video; the exact wording is made up.

At its core, an LLM does one thing: given some text, predict the next token (a token is a word or a piece of a word). It adds that token to the text and repeats. Press the button to watch it.

Concept 4

What is a "parameter"? The numbers on the wires

An LLM is a giant neural network. Every connection (wire) between two neurons has a number called a weight. Those weights are the parameters. Training means nudging these numbers so the network's predictions get better.

Picture itImagine a mixing desk with a billion tiny knobs. Training turns the knobs a little at a time until the music (the predictions) sounds right. Turning every knob is slow and needs a huge desk (GPU memory). That's the problem the next three ideas solve.
Concept 5

Full fine-tuning vs Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)

PEFT is like transfer learning in deep learning, where you freeze most layers and train only a few. The two most popular PEFT methods are LoRA and QLoRA, which are next.

Concept 6

LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation

Inside a transformer, the trainable parts are big matrices (grids of numbers) such as WQ, WK, WV, WO in attention and the matrices in the feed-forward layer. Call any one of them W.

Moving the rank slider also changes the waist of the green A/B shape in the diagram above. Common ranks are 8 or 16; higher means more capacity but more memory.

Two more knobs: lora_alpha and target_modules

lora_alpha controls how loudly the adapter "speaks". The real formula is h = W·x + (α / r)·B·A·x. With α = 16 and r = 16 (our settings) the scale is 1, so the adapter's change is added at normal strength.

target_modules chooses which matrices get an adapter. Llama has 16 identical layers, and each one contains these 7 matrices. We add LoRA to all of them:

If you leave a module out of target_modules, that matrix stays fully frozen with no adapter.

Concept 7

Quantization: storing each number with fewer bits

Every parameter is a number, and numbers take memory. Normally each one uses 32 bits (4 bytes), so a 7-billion-parameter model needs about 28 GB just to load. Quantization stores each number with fewer bits: 8 bits needs a quarter of the memory, and 4 bits needs an eighth.

Try it · how much memory does a model need?
Model size:

Weights only. Training needs extra room for activations and optimizer state, which is another reason to keep the model small.

How it works: map floats onto a small set of integers

Weights are usually small decimals between −1 and 1. To store them as 8-bit integers (−128 … 127), we stretch the range onto the integers:

1. Find the scale
scale = (x_max − x_min) / (q_max − q_min)
= (1 − (−1)) / (127 − (−128)) = 0.0078
2. Quantize, and later dequantize
q = round(x / scale) → −0.91 becomes −116
x ≈ q × scale → −116 × 0.0078 = −0.9098

You get back almost the original number. It's like zipping and unzipping a photo: a little detail is lost, but a lot of space is saved.

Orange dots are the original weights and purple dots are where they land after quantization. The fewer the bits, the bigger the jump, and the bigger the error.

NF4: smarter 4-bit bins for bell-shaped weights

4 bits give 16 possible values. If we make 16 equal-width bins, there's a problem: neural-network weights follow a bell curve (normal distribution), with most of them crowded near zero. A few middle bins would hold most of the weights, and those weights would get lumped together. NormalFloat-4 (NF4) instead makes bins that each hold the same share of weights (1/16 = 6.25%). The bins are narrow in the crowded middle and wide at the empty edges.

Concept 8

QLoRA = Quantized base model + LoRA adapters

QLoRA combines the two ideas. It compresses the frozen base model to 4 bits so it fits in memory, then trains small LoRA adapters on top of it. The QLoRA paper fine-tuned a 65-billion-parameter model on a single 48 GB GPU. In 32-bit that model would need about 260 GB.

65B model, 32-bit≈ 260 GB
→
Quantize to NF4≈ 32.5 GB, frozen
→
+ LoRA adapterssmall, trainable, kept in higher precision
→
Fine-tuneon one GPU

The paper uses three tricks:

1 · 4-bit NF4

The bell-curve-friendly 4-bit format from the previous section.

2 · Double quantization

Weights are quantized in blocks of 64, and each block keeps its own scale. That's millions of scales, so the scales get quantized as well, like zipping a zip file.

3 · Paged optimizers

GPUmemory full
⇄
CPU RAMoverflow

When the GPU runs out of memory, some data is moved out to normal RAM and back when needed, like paging in an operating system. This prevents out-of-memory crashes.

In our projectload_in_4bit=True loads Llama-3.2-1B in 4-bit (the "Q"), and get_peft_model(...) adds the LoRA adapters. That's QLoRA, and it runs on a free 15 GB Colab T4.
Concept 9

How training actually works

When we train on raw text, every chunk becomes many small "guess the next token" quizzes. The target is simply the input shifted one token to the left:

Model sees →The5layersabovethemodel
Must predict →5layersabovethemodelare
1. Batch of chunks8 pieces of your PDF
→
2. Predictguess every next token
→
3. Losshow wrong were the guesses?
→
4. Update A & B onlynudge adapter numbers to be less wrong
↻

One trip around this loop is one step. One full pass over all your chunks is one epoch.

What does "loss" mean?Loss measures how surprised the model is by the real next token, and lower is better. In our run it fell from 5.25 to about 2.5. Roughly speaking, the model went from being as unsure as if it were picking among ~190 words (e5.25) to only ~12 (e2.5).
Don't chase zeroA loss of 0 would mean the model memorised your PDF word for word. We want it to learn the style and vocabulary, not to copy the text.

Turn documents into a training dataset

seconds

Goal: PDF → clean text → bite-sized chunks → a table of training examples.

Try it · what chunking does to text
Pieces shorter than size ÷ 10 are dropped (the real code uses 1000 and 100).

  • read_file returns a list of text pieces. Pages that contain only images give "", thanks to or "".
  • chunk: " ".join(text.split()) squashes all line breaks and extra spaces into single spaces. The text is then cut every 1000 characters, and any piece of 100 characters or fewer is thrown away.
  • Chunks keep training examples short enough to fit in GPU memory. 1000 characters is roughly 200–250 tokens.
  • Each chunk becomes a row {"text": ..., "source": filename}, and Dataset.from_list turns the rows into a Hugging Face dataset.
  • Folders such as .ipynb_checkpoints have no known extension, so read_file returns [] and they're skipped.

Instead of always taking the top token, we sample: pick randomly, but weighted by probability. Three settings shape that choice. Play with them:

temperature: low values make the top choice dominate (safe but repetitive), high values flatten the bars (creative but risky). top_p: keep only the smallest set of top tokens whose chances add up to p and cut the rest. repetition_penalty: shrink the score of tokens already used ("layers" here), which stops loops.

Limits and where to go next

Limits of this approach

  • Raw-text training gives a text continuer, not a Q&A bot. It writes in the document's style rather than answering questions.
  • PDF extraction is imperfect: glued words, headers and footers end up in the training text.
  • Small model, short training. It learns style and vocabulary, but its facts are not reliable.
  • No formal evaluation. Results were checked by reading outputs, not measured with a benchmark.

Next steps

  • Turn chunks into question → answer pairs (instruction fine-tuning). This is what the video does with a reasoning dataset.
  • Clean the PDF text: fix glued words, remove headers and footers.
  • Hold back some chunks as a validation set and watch validation loss.
  • Combine with RAG for up-to-date facts.
  • Try a bigger model (Llama-3.2-3B) or export to GGUF to run locally.

Glossary

Words used on this page

Token
A word or a piece of a word. Models read and write tokens.
Parameter / weight
One number inside the network that training can adjust.
Base model
A model that only autocompletes text.
Instruct model
A base model fine-tuned to follow instructions and chat.
Fine-tuning
Extra training of a pre-trained model on your own data.
RAG
Retrieval-Augmented Generation: look up documents and paste them into the prompt, without retraining.
PEFT
Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning: freeze the model and train a small add-on.
LoRA
Low-Rank Adaptation: learn the change ΔW as two thin matrices, B × A.
Rank (r)
The width of the thin "waist" between A and B. Bigger means more capacity.
lora_alpha
Scales how strongly the adapter affects the output (α / r).
Quantization
Storing numbers with fewer bits (for example 4 instead of 32) to save memory.
NF4
4-bit NormalFloat, with bins shaped for bell-curve weights.
QLoRA
A 4-bit quantized frozen model with LoRA adapters trained on top.
Adapter
The saved LoRA weights: a small file that sits on top of the base model.
EOS token
A special "end of sequence" token that marks where text stops.
Step / epoch
A step is one weight update; an epoch is one full pass over the dataset.
Batch / gradient accumulation

How wrong (surprised) the model's predictions are. Lower is better.
Learning rate
How big each update step is.
Temperature / top_p
Generation settings that control how random or focused the next-token choice is.
Unsloth
A library that makes LoRA/QLoRA fine-tuning faster and lighter on memory.
SFTTrainer
Supervised Fine-Tuning trainer from Hugging Face TRL. It runs the training loop.
